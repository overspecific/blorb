# Australia and New Zealand

Australasia invented the biscuit designed to survive a war, and the one designed to be drunk through. Both achievements are permanent.

## ANZAC Biscuit

A hardy oat-and-golden-syrup biscuit (also coconut, sometimes) that became associated with WWI, when families and support groups baked and shipped them to soldiers overseas because the ingredients didn't spoil on the long voyage. It has a protected name in Australia: commercial products can't be called an ANZAC biscuit without approval. Coconut inclusion is standard; raisin inclusion is a crime. Eaten on Anzac Day (25 April), sold year-round, and claimed jointly by both nations.

## Tim Tam

Two chocolate-malt biscuit layers with chocolate cream, entirely enrobed in chocolate, launched by Arnott's in 1964. The Tim Tam Slam — biting opposite corners and using the biscuit as a straw for hot tea, coffee, or milk, then eating the whole thing before it collapses — is the correct way to eat one, and is endorsed by the manufacturer.

## Lamington

A sponge square dipped in chocolate icing and rolled in desiccated coconut, named after Lord Lamington, Governor of Queensland. Both Australia and New Zealand claim it; the argument is eternal. It sits at the boundary of the biscuit kingdom (it's really a cake), but is included for completeness and because the argument is fun.

## Afghan Biscuit

A New Zealand classic: a chocolate-and-cornflake biscuit topped with chocolate icing and half a walnut, with no known connection to Afghanistan. The cornflakes are the signature — they give the biscuit its distinctive crunch.
