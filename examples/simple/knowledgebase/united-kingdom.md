# United Kingdom

The spiritual home of the biscuit-as-institution: the biscuit tin, the biscuit break, and the dunk.

## Digestive

A semi-sweet, lightly wheaty biscuit invented in 1839 in Scotland, with McVitie's beginning large-scale production in 1892. The name comes from the Victorian belief that its baking soda aided digestion; it does not, but the name stuck. The plain digestive is a sturdy, understated performer, but its true significance is as the base of the chocolate digestive (introduced 1925): the UK's most-dunked biscuit. The moral debate about chocolate-side-up or chocolate-side-down remains unresolved, and both sides have strong opinions about the other side's opinions.

## Rich Tea

The plainest biscuit in the tin and, by design, the best dunker in common circulation. Dry, faintly sweet, structurally engineered to absorb tea for an impressively long time before surrendering. If a biscuit could be described as "dependable" and mean it as high praise, this is the one.

## Custard Cream

A sandwich biscuit with a vanilla "custard" buttercream filling between two ornately embossed bars; the embossing design has barely changed since Victorian times. Was voted "Britain's favourite biscuit" in a 2007 reader poll and has never let the country forget it. The correct way to eat one is to twist off the top, scrape the filling, and then pretend you didn't.

## Bourbon

A chocolate sandwich biscuit introduced in 1910 by Peek Frean, whose factory also gave the world the Garibaldi. The classic technique is to suck the cream out through the tiny holes in the corners while maintaining eye contact with anyone who objects.

## Jammie Dodger

Two shortbread rounds with a heart-shaped jam window, launched in 1960 by Burton's and named after a Beano character (Jemmy Dodger, a parody of the Artful Dodger). The jam is raspberry. It has always been raspberry.

## Ginger Nut

The hardest biscuit in common circulation, held together by ginger and sheer will. Excellent dunked, for the brave; catastrophic for the overconfident: it will snap, not bend.

## Hobnob

An oaty, chewier cousin of the digestive, launched in 1985 (the name is an old word for socialising). Available milk-chocolate-covered or plain; the plain surface texture is optimised for maximum tea transfer, making the chocolate variant a structural gamble that many take anyway.

## Shortbread

Scotland's finest export: butter, sugar, flour, and no apologies. The "short" refers to the high butterfat content that makes it crumble; traditional shapes are petticoat tails, fingers, and rounds. Not a dunking biscuit. See the dunking guide, but the short version is: some things are sacred.
