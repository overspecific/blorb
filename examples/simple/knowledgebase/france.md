# France

France is technically a cake-with-tea culture, but its biscuit output is formidable and mostly butter-driven.

## Madeleine

A small shell-shaped sponge cake-biscuit hybrid from Commercy, associated with an 18th-century cook named Madeleine and, more famously, with Proust: dunked in tea, one triggered his most famous involuntary memory in "In Search of Lost Time". Best eaten fresh from the oven, when the signature hump is at its proudest. Day-old madeleines are best in cake terms: dunked in chocolate.

## Langue de Chat ("cat's tongue")

Thin, buttery, elongated biscuits, crisp enough to snap cleanly. Often paired with chocolate (dipped, sandwiched, or alongside sorbet) and used as an edible garnish in patisserie. Elegance is the whole point; they do not keep well, which is considered correct.

## Palet Breton

A dense, crumbly, salted-butter shortbread from Brittany. Thick, round, and unreasonably rich: the salted butter tradition of Brittany (beurre salé) in biscuit form. One is a treat; three is a commitment.

## Petit Beurre

The plain butter biscuit standardised by Lefèvre-Utile of Nantes in 1886, with the "LU" embossed on the surface. It has four ears ("oreillettes"), 52 teeth, and 24 pinpricks, which fans insist encode the four seasons, 52 weeks, and 24 hours of the day. Whether that's true or a beautiful accident, it tells you everything about how seriously France takes a plain biscuit.
