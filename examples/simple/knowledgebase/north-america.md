# North America

America runs on the cookie — soft, chunky, and dessert-leaning by British biscuit standards. The crisp/dry end of the spectrum is where the two traditions meet.

## Chocolate Chip Cookie

Invented by Ruth Wakefield at the Toll House Inn, Whitman, Massachusetts, in 1938: she chopped a Nestlé chocolate bar into butter cookie dough expecting it to melt, and got chips instead. Nestlé printed her recipe on their packaging in exchange for a lifetime chocolate supply. Whether it counts as a "biscuit" in the British sense is a permanent transatlantic border dispute. Soft or crisp is a personal moral test.

## Snickerdoodle

A cinnamon-sugar-dusted cookie with a tang from cream of tartar, rolled in cinnamon sugar before baking. The name possibly comes from German Schnecken ("snails"), via Pennsylvania Dutch country, but nobody is sure. The name means nothing; the biscuit does not care.

## Oreo

Two chocolate wafer discs with a sweet vanilla filling, launched by Nabisco in 1912 and now the best-selling cookie on Earth. Twist-and-lick is the advertised ritual; the correct method remains contested. Oreos are the backbone of the biscuit-adjacent dessert canon: cheesecake bases, milkshakes, deep-fried state fair items.

## Graham Cracker

A mildly sweet, honey-flavoured whole-wheat biscuit invented in 1829 by Sylvester Graham, a Presbyterian minister who believed bland food suppressed impure thoughts. Modern America has fully repurposed his anti-pleasure biscuit into s'mores, cheesecake bases, and pie crusts, which is the funniest possible outcome.

## Fig Newton

A soft, square pastry filled with fig paste, from Newton, Massachusetts — the name is a rare example of a biscuit named after its actual origin town. One of the earliest commercially baked products in America (1891) and still sold today, largely to people who remember liking them more than they did.
