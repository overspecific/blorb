# Italy

Italy's biscuit tradition is ancient and mostly almond-driven. "Biscotto" means "twice-cooked", which is also where the English word biscuit comes from.

## Biscotti (Cantucci)

Twice-baked almond biscuits from Prato, cut from a loaf and baked again precisely to make them hard enough to survive storage — and to be dunked in Vin Santo, which is not optional so much as the intended serving method. Refusing is rude.

## Amaretti

Small, crisp almond macarons with a chewier middle, associated with Saronno; one legend credits a local innkeeper with inventing them for a visiting cardinal. Made with bitter almonds (amare means bitter), which give them their distinctive fragrance. Naturally gluten-free, which the Romans did not know but would have taken credit for.

## Savoiardi (Ladyfingers)

Light, dry, sponge-like fingers named after the court of Savoy, where they were baked to impress visiting dignitaries in the late Middle Ages. Their entire purpose is absorption: the structural basis of tiramisu and charlottes. In Britain they arrive in trifle; in Italy they are treated with respect.

## Baci di Dama ("lady's kisses")

Two small hazelnut- or almond-flavoured domes sandwiched around a layer of dark chocolate, so that the seam looks like a kiss. From Piedmont, where chocolate and hazelnuts have a long working relationship. Excellent with coffee, which is also Piedmont's view on most things.

## Pan di Stelle

Star-studded cocoa-and-hazelnut breakfast biscuits from Mulino Bianco, beloved by Italian children and nostalgic adults alike. They are specifically shaped for dunking in milk at breakfast, and the marketing material specifies this. The stars are, allegedly, inspired by dreams of astronauts. Italy takes breakfast biscuits to places others do not.
